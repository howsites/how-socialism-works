03 · The basics

One question underneath it all

Every economy has to decide who owns the means of production: the land, factories, tools, and firms that produce goods and services.

Under capitalism, they're mostly privately owned, and owners keep the profits. Socialism is a family of ideas in which they're owned or controlled socially (by the state, by workers, or by communities), with the goal of sharing what's produced more equally.

04 · Core ideas

Four pillars

1

Social ownership

Key industries belong to the public, to cooperatives, or to the people who work in them, not to private shareholders.

2

Production for use

Goods are made because people need them, not mainly because they turn a profit.

3

Economic democracy

Workers and citizens get a say in economic decisions, from the shop floor up to national priorities.

4

Greater equality

Income and wealth are spread more evenly, and essentials like health care and education are guaranteed.

05 · The mechanics

Three ways to run it

Socialists disagree, sometimes sharply, about how to put these ideas into practice. Here are the main models.

Central planning

A government agency decides what to produce, how much, and at what price, usually through multi-year plans. The Soviet Union ran this way from 1928 until 1991.

Market socialism

Firms are owned socially, often by their workers, but they still buy and sell in markets. Yugoslavia tried versions of this, and cooperatives like Spain's Mondragon work this way today.

Workers / public

Social democracy

Not socialism in the strict sense: most firms stay private. Instead, high taxes pay for universal welfare, public services, and strong unions. The Nordic countries are the usual example.

06 · A short history

From pamphlet to policy

  1. 1848

    Marx and Engels publish The Communist Manifesto.

  2. 1864

    The First International brings together workers' movements from across Europe.

  3. 1917

    The Russian Revolution leads to the first state to call itself socialist.

  4. 1948

    Britain creates the National Health Service as part of a postwar wave of nationalization.

  5. 1950

    Yugoslavia introduces workers' self-management of firms.

  6. 1956

    The Mondragon cooperative is founded in Spain's Basque Country.

  7. 1978

    China begins market reforms under Deng Xiaoping.

  8. 1991

    The Soviet Union dissolves, ending the largest centrally planned economy.

07 · The debate

Arguments on both sides

Supporters argue

  • It reduces inequality and poverty.
  • It gives workers a voice in their own workplaces.
  • It provides public goods that markets underprovide.
  • It protects essentials from boom-and-bust cycles.

Critics argue

  • Without market prices, planners can't calculate efficiently (the "calculation problem" raised by Mises and Hayek).
  • It weakens incentives to work and innovate.
  • It concentrates power in the state.
  • Planned economies have seen shortages and repression.

08 · A real-world test

The story of Post Modern Times

In 2026, a Minneapolis café tried running without prices. In six steps, here's what happened.

  1. Before

    Running on thin margins

    Modern Times, a Minneapolis café open for about 16 years, had barely broken even since 2020. The owner says 2025 brought $1.3M in sales and an $18,500 loss.

  2. Jan 2026

    Prices disappear

    Renamed Post Modern Times, it drops prices. Meals run on donations, and staff work for shared tips. The owner calls it “market socialism.”

  3. Spring

    Everyone shows up

    Over 30,000 meals go out on about 2,000 donations. Lines, crowds and arguments strain the neighborhood.

  4. Summer

    Goodwill runs low

    An early wave of over half a million dollars in donations fades. In July, after six months, the café pauses.

  5. August

    Prices come back

    It reopens with prices on the menu again.

  6. Oct 2026

    Closing time

    The owner announces the café will close for good at the end of 2026.

The lesson in miniature

When food cost nothing, demand soared, but the bills for ingredients, rent and staff didn't go away. Without prices, the café ran on goodwill, and goodwill ran low. It's a small-scale version of the problem critics see in socialism: someone still has to pay, and when no one has to, fewer people do.

To be fair: the owner hasn't directly blamed the closure on the experiment, and some figures are his own. Sources: Star Tribune (pause), Star Tribune (closing), NewsChannel 9.
